# Positioning Claim Gates

Apply these gates when writing positioning statements, value propositions, messaging angles, and taglines. They convert strategic identity into claims a buyer can picture and price.

## Value currency

Never frame the offer primarily as saving time or saving money. Frame it as creating more of what the buyer's own scoreboard counts: revenue, leads, deals closed, customers retained, incidents prevented. A mechanism pitch (how it works) and a deliverable pitch (what arrives) are feature claims: demote them to the product level and find the scoreboard for the promise.

Frame savings only for a reader who reports the saved number upward and is paid on that result. One qualifying role does not license a savings pitch aimed at the colleague who signs.

## Level fit

Place each claim at the right level of the customer's needs ladder, where each need serves the end one level up:

- **Product level:** features and specifics. A buyer searching at this level wants the spec, not lifestyle promises.
- **One level up:** the promise. One level only; claiming further levels loses credibility.
- **Far above:** aspiration and proof that it happens, never a promise.
- **Below:** what the product makes obsolete. Name the removed steps vividly.
- **Vendors above:** counter with what the customer keeps by staying at this level.

An audience filter is not a level. Narrowing belongs inside a claim, such as "for post-Series-A healthcare companies facing their first SOC 2", never as a standalone statement of who the message is for.

## One price story

Every statement tells one self-consistent pricing story:

- **More for More:** best at something nameable, for buyers who care about that thing more than price.
- **More for Less:** the best the customer can afford; name the trade-offs plainly instead of claiming best for everyone.
- **Less for Less:** minimal and incredibly affordable; proudly name the missing amenities that make the price possible.

A price can appear in any story. An affordability identity, such as "cheapest in the industry", may not mix with another story.

## Opposite Test

Construct each claim's opposite. If no successful company would rationally choose the opposite, the claim is trivial and dies. It passes when the opposite is a real strategy someone pursues, ideally a named competitor.

Exception: a generic claim such as "fastest" is ownable when the gap is measured and dramatic (a multiple, not a margin), central to why the ideal customer buys, and the owner commits to leading every use with current evidence. The exception never resurrects dead clichés.

## Specificity

- Replace generic words with the specific thing they stand for.
- Cut clichés; every competitor claims them and nobody believes them.
- Show proof instead of stating adjectives: the benchmark, the video, the changelog, the warranty.
- Prefer clauses that narrow a claim's scope. Test by deleting the conjunction and everything after it: if the claim gets sharper, the clause was diluting. Accuracy outranks narrowing; a true range is not padding.

## Truth gate

Never invent a number, benchmark, testimonial, rating, or story. Every specific is supplied or confirmed by the user. A rewrite that needs an unavailable fact takes a bracketed placeholder plus a facts-to-verify line, and is not final until the fact lands. A confirmed truth worded to imply more than it says fails the gate. This gate outranks vividness.
